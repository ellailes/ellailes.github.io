Research

Axion and Dark Photon Production in Magnetized White Dwarfs

Axions and dark photons are among the most well-motivated extensions of the Standard Model, and their production can be efficient in the hot, dense plasmas inside stars. Almost all of the existing stellar limits on these particles, though, assume an unmagnetized, isotropic plasma. In this work we showed that the photon self-energy in a magnetized plasma is qualitatively very different than an unmagnetized one, primarily because of the local anisotropy imparted by the background field. We considered magnetic white dwarfs as a case study and found that in large volumes of these stars, resonant production of beyond-Standard-Model particles can be enabled purely by the presence of a magnetic field, with multiple plasma modes contributing simultaneously at different radii. We found that magnetic fields change the energy and regions of dark photon and axion production, increasing the regions where the production of these particles is allowed and changing the energies of emitted particles. The local emissivity is in some limits very different from what one would obtain using unmagnetized dispersion relations, which motivates revisiting astrophysical constraints on light bosons in any system where the cyclotron frequency is comparable to or larger than the plasma frequency.

Direct Detection of the Millicharged Background

Millicharged particles (MCPs) carry a tiny effective charge through their coupling to a kinetically mixed dark photon, which makes them a key benchmark for collider and astronomical searches. These particles are usually assumed to be hard to detect unless they make up a large share of the dark matter. Our starting point was that if MCPs exist as particle states in the spectrum, then some abundance will inevitably be produced in the early universe through freeze-in, as annihilations and decays of Standard Model states generate a stable density of them. That gives an irreducible background, so we recast existing forecasts and constraints that assume MCPs are all of the dark matter into statements about their mere existence. We show that ongoing and proposed direct detection experiments will have world-leading sensitivity to effective charges of Q ∼ 10−12 across nine orders of magnitude in mass, corresponding to a dark matter sub-fraction as low as ∼10−3 — meaning these experiments are sensitive to MCPs even when they are a tiny sub-fraction of the dark matter.

Novel Statistical Estimators for Line Intensity Mapping

Line intensity mapping (LIM) uses spectral lines emitted from galaxies and other regions as a way to learn about matter in the universe at a variety of redshifts. The auto power spectrum of these spectral lines is a useful quantity but the data is often limited by instrumental noise, residual foregrounds, and systematics. Cross-power spectra between multiple lines offer a robust alternative to infer the auto power spectrum. Inferring the auto spectrum from cross-correlations relies on two key assumptions: that all tracers are linearly biased with respect to the matter density field, and that they are strongly mutually correlated. In this work we introduced a new diagnostic statistic, Q, built from combinations of cross-spectra between four different spectral lines, which serves as a data-driven null test of those assumptions. We validated it on both analytic toy models and simulations of LIM observables across a range of redshifts and noise. We found that the criterion Q ≈ 1 reliably selects the regions where cross-spectrum estimators are valid, while significant deviations indicate that the key assumptions have been violated. This gives astronomers a convenient way to define the scale cuts and redshift ranges over which these reconstructions can be trusted.
